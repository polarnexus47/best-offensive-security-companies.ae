How to Choose an Offensive Security Company in the UAE

Choose by evidence, not by logo. Decide first whether you need depth (a boutique specialist), compliance mapping (an offensive-led consultancy) or continuous monitoring (a managed-security-led provider). Then send two or three providers the same written scope and compare their methodology, sample report, retest terms, named testers and stated exclusions. Verify every accreditation in the issuing directory before you sign.

Neither model is better in every case. A boutique team can test deeply and leave monitoring to someone else. A managed provider can fold testing into one contract. The ranking uses these models as tiers, so you can read how the companies compare.

Four steps keep the process short. First, write the scope and the reason for the test. Second, shortlist two or three providers from the ranking. Third, send the checklist below with the scope. Fourth, compare answers side by side and ask the finalists for a sample report and a call with the lead tester.

What a public profile tells you before the first call. Four signals on a provider's own site are worth checking:

  • a named testing methodology or standard on the service pages;
  • a description of report contents: proof of concept, severity scoring, remediation guidance;
  • stated test types, such as web, mobile, API, network, cloud, wireless or red team;
  • a stated UAE presence or a reference to a UAE programme such as Dubai Cyber Force.

If a profile states none of these, the RFP questions below carry more weight, because the answers are the only evidence you will have.

Ten pass or fail questions for your RFP

Send these in writing. Treat a missing or vague answer as a fail.

  1. Methodology named. Which methodology do you follow (OWASP WSTG, NIST SP 800-115, PTES, MITRE ATT&CK)? Pass: a named method referenced in the report.
  2. Report sample. Can I see a sanitised sample report? Pass: a real sample with findings, proof of concept, CVSS scores and remediation steps.
  3. Retest. Is a retest included, and how many rounds? Pass: stated in the quote.
  4. Named practitioners. Who will perform the work, and what is their role? Pass: named roles and relevant credentials for the people assigned.
  5. Scope exclusions. What will you not test or not cover? Pass: a written list.
  6. Communication cadence. How will you report progress and urgent findings? Pass: a stated channel and a rule for critical findings.
  7. Data handling. Where is my data stored, who can access it, how long is it kept, and how is it destroyed? Pass: a written answer.
  8. Insurance. Do you carry professional indemnity or cyber liability insurance covering testing? Pass: confirmation on request.
  9. References. Can you provide references for similar engagements? Pass: references or a stated reason why not, such as client confidentiality.
  10. Quote basis. What drives the price: assets, roles, depth, retest, reporting? Pass: the quote lists the basis. See penetration testing cost in the UAE.

Six red flags

  • A guarantee of zero vulnerabilities. No test proves absence. A test reports what it found within its scope and time.
  • Scanner output sold as a penetration test. If the report is a list of tool results with no exploitation or proof of concept, it is a vulnerability assessment.
  • No scope document. Testing without a signed scope and rules of engagement is a legal and operational risk.
  • No retest. Without a retest, you cannot show which findings were closed, and an auditor or customer will ask.
  • Unnamed testers. If the provider cannot say who will work on your project, depth is unknowable.
  • An accreditation you cannot verify. A logo without a directory entry is a claim, not a proof.

When you score the answers, give extra weight to items 1 to 5. Methodology, a real sample report, a retest, named testers and stated exclusions predict the quality of the work. Items 6 to 10 reduce operational and commercial risk, but they do not show testing depth.

Three different proofs

A company accreditation shows that an organisation passed an assessment by an issuing body. An individual certification shows that a person passed an exam. Real experience shows what the people assigned to your project have done before. These are different proofs, and none replaces the others. Ask for all three, and read the guide to certifications and accreditations to see what each one does and does not show.

Check the claims in the provider's own profile against its public site. If the ranking shows "Not stated" for a field you care about, such as accreditation or report contents, ask the provider to state it in writing before you proceed.

What to request for each proof:

  • Company accreditation: the directory entry and the services the accreditation covers.
  • Individual certification: the holder's name and the role that person will play on your project.
  • Experience: a description of comparable engagements, without client names, and the lead tester's part in each.
