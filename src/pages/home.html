What counts as offensive security

Offensive security is the practice of attacking your own systems, with authorisation, to find exploitable weaknesses before a real attacker does. Offensive security companies sell that work as a service: scoped tests, a written report and a retest.

Included in this ranking

  • Vulnerability assessment and penetration testing (VAPT)authorised exploitation of defined targets, with evidence.
  • Red teaminga goal-driven simulated attack against people, process and technology.
  • Purple teamingattackers and defenders working together to tune detection.
  • Social engineeringphishing and pretext tests against staff.
  • Application and API testingweb, mobile and API logic and access control.
  • Cloud and wireless testingconfiguration and network exposure outside the office perimeter.

Not included

  • SOC-as-a-service and managed detection and response sold as the main product.
  • Product resale and licence reselling.
  • Compliance-only consulting with no hands-on testing.

Companies in the third tier sell managed detection or SOC services as their main product and offer testing as an additional line. They are included because they publicly describe offensive services, and they are ranked below specialists because offensive work is not the whole offering.

Seven questions to ask before you sign

Ask each shortlisted provider these questions in writing. A clear answer is a pass; an evasive answer is a fail.

  1. Which methodology do you follow, and can you name it (for example OWASP WSTG or NIST SP 800-115)? A named method makes the work repeatable and auditable.
  2. What does the report contain: proof of concept, CVSS score, remediation steps? Without evidence, findings cannot be reproduced or fixed.
  3. Is a retest included, and how many rounds? A retest confirms the fixes actually closed the findings.
  4. Who performs the work, and which testers will be on my project? Depth depends on the people, not the brand.
  5. Which parts of my scope will you not cover? Every test has limits, and a provider should state them.
  6. What rules of engagement and scope exclusions will we sign? They protect production systems and define what is authorised.
  7. Which accreditations do you hold, and where can I verify them? Check the issuing directory, not the logo on a website.
